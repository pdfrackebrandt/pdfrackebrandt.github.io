I'm an incoming MPhil Economics student at the University of Oxford, at Merton College, continuing to the DPhil. I study why constitutions fail.

Not why governments fall. Why the rulebook itself stops being worth following, decades after everyone agreed to it. I build mathematical models of when that happens, and test them against Latin America, where constitutions are rewritten often enough to measure how long they last.

What this is. Think of a country's politics as a wobble. Parties swap, crises arrive, protests happen, and normally things settle back down. The blue line is that wobble, traced out over decades. A wide loop means big political swings. A tight loop means calm.

Why it climbs. The line rises because the country's birth rate is falling, and that is the only thing changing here. Fewer children means the average voter keeps getting older, and older voters want different things from a constitution than younger ones do.

Low down, the loop shrinks to nothing. A crisis hits, the system absorbs it, life returns to normal. That is a constitution doing its job.

Higher up it stops shrinking. Past the dashed square the loop widens instead, and never closes again. A crisis no longer fades. The country swings from one kind of government to the next, then the next, and keeps going round. Rome did this. So has most of Latin America.

Test it yourself. Press Shock it while the dot is near the bottom and the wobble dies out. Press it near the top and it never does. That difference is the entire argument.

What the model actually is

The normal form of a Hopf bifurcation, integrated live. Horizontal axes are the two state variables; the vertical axis is θ, the speed of the demographic transition, which drifts slowly upward so the system passes through the bifurcation rather than sitting at a fixed value. For θ < 0 the origin is a stable spiral. At θ = 0 a pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis and the origin loses stability, throwing off a limit cycle of radius √θ. The trajectory settles onto that radius only after a lag, the delay effect characteristic of slow passage through a bifurcation. These are the equations from paper 00.

Research

00

Why regimes go round in circles

Democracies decay into something worse, and so does whatever replaces them. Polybius noticed the loop in 150 BC. I show it is what the mathematics predicts once a country's birth rate falls fast enough, so the cycle is a property of the system and not a run of bad luck.

The technical version

Regime cycling recovered as a limit cycle in a dynamic political economy model, with the speed of the demographic transition θ as the bifurcation parameter. Below θ*, the polity spirals into a stable constitutional equilibrium. Above it, that equilibrium loses stability through a Hopf bifurcation and the regime sequence turns periodic. Tested against V-Dem.

01

Why no constitution can do everything

Write down five things you want from a constitution: it should be stable, hard to capture, open to newcomers, able to bind the future, and treat everyone alike. You cannot have all five. I prove that, then find the design that gives up the least.

The technical version

Five axioms (stability, non-capture, openness, commitment efficiency, anonymity) shown mutually incompatible in an overlapping-generations economy with heterogeneous patience and multidimensional policy. Epistocratic bicameralism characterised as the unique escape. A companion convergence result shows the constitution self-obsolesces as the patience distribution shifts.

02

Checking it against Latin America

Theory is cheap. Latin America has rewritten its constitutions often enough that you can actually count how long they survive, so I use near-tied elections as a natural experiment to see whether the prediction holds.

The technical version

Regression discontinuity around constitutional reform thresholds, using close elections and the bifurcation threshold itself as cutoffs, testing whether the cycling predicted by 00 appears as a discontinuity in constitutional survival.
