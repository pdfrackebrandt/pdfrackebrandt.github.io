MPhil in Economics, University of Oxford, Merton. Undergraduate dissertation at the University of Manchester on volatility as a measure of price dislocation in commodity markets. Later worked in brokerage at a family office.

I study the incentive structures of hybrid regimes. Superintelligence will let agents handle nearly any task that can be automated, which risks dissolving the social contract between elites and everyone else under democratic systems. Bad or good, we must adapt. How?

In my view there are two cases, with regional variants. a) technology outgrows gov debt, b) it doesn't. Unless a) is large enough to achieve post economic abundance, questions of legitimacy will arrive. For such optimisation, is that I'd love to answer the how to implement a given regime, under what conditions, and for which society.

Aristotle and Polybius traced how constitutions decay and settled on the mixed republic, a "politeia" balancing claims of wealth and power against free birth, more resistant to degeneration over time. Interestingly, when a) is solved, this directionally motivates for research into multiplanetary civilisation optimisation. Best time to be alive!

When regimes cycle

1 / 3A shock fades

drag to rotate

Many countries swing between democracy and autocracy for decades. Why? I model two forces chasing each other. Young people organise against the rulers, who open up institutions to absorb them because that pays. Absorbing them drains the protest, so openness stops paying and the rulers close again. Then a new generation comes of age and the loop starts over; a cycle.

Read moreRead less

Whether the cycle lasts depends on the pool of young people free to mobilise. Below a threshold, it fades. Above it, it never stops. What then moves a country across? Falling behind its neighbours fills the pool of young people and lifts a country over. A fall in real income makes open institutions costlier to keep, which raises the threshold and drops the country below.

The threshold says whether the cycle runs, not which way a regime moves. That depends on the initial regime type, and only young regimes move because old ones have consolidated. This gives three predictions.

  1. A young autocracy that falls behind its neighbours enters the cycle and opens up.
  2. A fall in real income keeps autocracies shut and closes young democracies.
  3. A neighbour turning democratic pulls a new autocracy toward opening, unless it has already consolidated.

All three are statistically significant in V-Dem data on nearly every country from 1950 to 2019. Each pattern was known on its own, and so the model explains all three in one!

How many tests was it really?

with Sebastian Winzker

Why does this matter? When you test thousands of trading rules, the best one will look good by chance. Cluster corrections that group similar rules can make noise look significant by undercounting the tests. A block bootstrap on the real grid keeps the correlations and shows the safer hurdle is the full nominal count.

We resample the 2,825 moving average rules together. On the DAX the effective independent tests are about 1,400. Correlation spread evenly barely lowers that number; only rules bunched in blocks do. The best rule still fails the hurdle on the DAX.

Reversed by our own replication

Why does this matter? An automated auditor that checks research extractions is right only about a third of the time, yet a wrong extraction can still return a theorem. We registered three signals in advance that might catch the error, then tested them once on frontier models. Two reversed. Cross model disagreement is the one that held.

On 174 items, a model disagreeing with its own repeats cleared the bar in only one family. Disagreement across models reached 0.75 and stayed above the threshold. It flagged errors better on unconventional designs. Abstaining where models disagree most trims the error rate from 81% to 72%, but the tool remains weak.
