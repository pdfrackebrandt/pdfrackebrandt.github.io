In short

  • Probability is a rule for weighing events: never negative, total weight 1, disjoint events add. Everything below follows from those three lines.
  • A distribution is pinned down by its CDF. The density is the slope of the CDF, or the size of its jumps.
  • Moments summarise a distribution. Conditioning splits them: the overall mean is an average of conditional means, and total spread is spread within groups plus spread between them.
  • Independence implies mean independence, which implies zero covariance. The arrows do not run backwards, except under joint normality. This chain is the backbone of every OLS assumption to come.

1. Random variables and the axioms

A random variable XXX turns each uncertain outcome into a number. A discrete variable takes finitely or countably many values. A continuous variable takes values in an interval, and the chance of hitting any single point is zero: P(X=x)=0P(X = x) = 0P(X=x)=0 for every xxx.

Kolmogorov's axioms. Let Ω\OmegaΩ be the set of all outcomes and AAA any event, a subset of Ω\OmegaΩ:

  • Nonnegativity: P(A)≥0P(A) \ge 0P(A)≥0
  • Normalisation: P(Ω)=1P(\Omega) = 1P(Ω)=1
  • Countable additivity: if A1,A2,…A_1, A_2, \dotsA1​,A2​,… are pairwise disjoint, then P(⋃iAi)=∑iP(Ai)P\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big) = \sum_i P(A_i)P(⋃i​Ai​)=∑i​P(Ai​)

Two rules follow at once. Split BBB into the part inside AAA and the part outside: P(B)=P(A∩B)+P(B∖A)P(B) = P(A \cap B) + P(B \setminus A)P(B)=P(A∩B)+P(B∖A). Adding P(A)P(A)P(A) to both sides gives the union rule

P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)

The overlap is counted twice, so it is subtracted once. And if A⊂BA \subset BA⊂B, then P(B)=P(A)+P(B∖A)≥P(A)P(B) = P(A) + P(B \setminus A) \ge P(A)P(B)=P(A)+P(B∖A)≥P(A). Every event sits inside Ω\OmegaΩ, so P(A)≤1P(A) \le 1P(A)≤1.

Takeaway

Probability is never negative, something always happens, and disjoint events add. Monotonicity and the union rule are consequences, not extra assumptions.

2. CDFs and densities

The cumulative distribution function F(x)=P(X≤x)F(x) = P(X \le x)F(x)=P(X≤x) exists for every random variable: discrete, continuous or mixed. It always has three properties:

  • F(x)→0F(x) \to 0F(x)→0 as x→−∞x \to -\inftyx→−∞ and F(x)→1F(x) \to 1F(x)→1 as x→∞x \to \inftyx→∞
  • FFF never decreases
  • FFF is right continuous

Why right continuous? As xxx falls towards x0x_0x0​ from above, the events {X≤x}\{X \le x\}{X≤x} shrink to {X≤x0}\{X \le x_0\}{X≤x0​}, and probability is continuous along shrinking events. From the left, {X≤x}\{X \le x\}{X≤x} grows only to {X<x0}\{X < x_0\}{X<x0​}, which misses the point x0x_0x0​ itself. So FFF can jump at x0x_0x0​, and the jump is exactly the probability sitting there:

P(X=x0)=F(x0)−lim⁡x↑x0F(x)P(a<X≤b)=F(b)−F(a)\begin{aligned} P(X = x_0) &= F(x_0) - \lim_{x \uparrow x_0} F(x) \\ P(a < X \le b) &= F(b) - F(a) \end{aligned}P(X=x0​)P(a<X≤b)​=F(x0​)−x↑x0​lim​F(x)=F(b)−F(a)​

The density describes the same distribution locally. Discrete: the probability mass function p(x)=P(X=x)p(x) = P(X = x)p(x)=P(X=x), the jumps of FFF. Continuous: f(x)=F′(x)f(x) = F'(x)f(x)=F′(x) wherever the derivative exists, so F(x)=∫−∞xf(t) dtF(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dtF(x)=∫−∞x​f(t)dt.

Intuition

A density is probability per unit length, like population per square kilometre. It can exceed 1: Uniform(0,12)(0, \tfrac12)(0,21​) has f(x)=2f(x) = 2f(x)=2 on its support, yet the area under it is still 1.

Takeaway

The density is the slope of the CDF (continuous) or its jumps (discrete). Whether an interval includes its endpoints matters only where there is an atom.

4. Moments, iterated expectations and total variance

The kkkth moment is E[Xk]E[X^k]E[Xk]. It exists only if E[∣X∣k]<∞E\big[|X|^k\big] < \inftyE[∣X∣k]<∞. Without absolute convergence the sum or integral can depend on the order in which it is taken, and then it means nothing. The Cauchy distribution has no mean for this reason.

Variance measures spread around the mean μ=E[X]\mu = E[X]μ=E[X]. Expanding the square:

Var⁡(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−2μE[X]+μ2=E[X2]−μ2\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] \\ &= E[X^2] - 2\mu E[X] + \mu^2 = E[X^2] - \mu^2 \end{aligned}Var(X)​=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−2μE[X]+μ2=E[X2]−μ2​

Shifting XXX leaves the spread alone and scaling multiplies it: Var⁡(a+bX)=b2Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(a + bX) = b^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)Var(a+bX)=b2Var(X). For a Bernoulli, Var⁡(X)=p−p2=p(1−p)\operatorname{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = p(1-p)Var(X)=p−p2=p(1−p), largest at p=12p = \tfrac12p=21​, when the outcome is least predictable.

Iterated expectations

E[Y∣X]E[Y \mid X]E[Y∣X] is the mean of YYY once XXX is known. Before XXX is drawn it is itself a random variable, a function of XXX. Averaging it over XXX gives back the plain mean:

E[Y]=E[E[Y∣X]]E[Y] = E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big]E[Y]=E[E[Y∣X]]

For discrete variables the proof is a regrouping of one double sum:

E[E[Y∣X]]=∑x∑yy P(Y=y∣X=x) P(X=x)=∑yy∑xP(Y=y,X=x)=∑yy P(Y=y)=E[Y]\begin{aligned} &E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] \\ &= \sum_x \sum_y y\, P(Y = y \mid X = x)\, P(X = x) \\ &= \sum_y y \sum_x P(Y = y, X = x) \\ &= \sum_y y\, P(Y = y) = E[Y] \end{aligned}​E[E[Y∣X]]=x∑​y∑​yP(Y=y∣X=x)P(X=x)=y∑​yx∑​P(Y=y,X=x)=y∑​yP(Y=y)=E[Y]​

Intuition

A school's average grade is the average of its class averages, each weighted by class size. Conditioning first and averaging after loses nothing.

Total variance

Write the deviation of YYY from its mean in two pieces: the part XXX cannot explain, and the part it can.

Y−E[Y]=(Y−E[Y∣X])+(E[Y∣X]−E[Y])\begin{aligned} Y - E[Y] = {}&\big(Y - E[Y \mid X]\big) \\ &+ \big(E[Y \mid X] - E[Y]\big) \end{aligned}Y−E[Y]=​(Y−E[Y∣X])+(E[Y∣X]−E[Y])​

Square both sides and take expectations. The cross term has mean zero: once XXX is known the second bracket is a fixed number, and the first averages to zero. What is left:

Var⁡(Y)=E[Var⁡(Y∣X)]⏟within+Var⁡(E[Y∣X])⏟between\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \underbrace{E\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big]}_{\text{within}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(E[Y \mid X]\big)}_{\text{between}}Var(Y)=withinE[Var(Y∣X)]​​+betweenVar(E[Y∣X])​​

The between share, Var⁡(E[Y∣X])/Var⁡(Y)\operatorname{Var}(E[Y \mid X]) / \operatorname{Var}(Y)Var(E[Y∣X])/Var(Y), is the population R2R^2R2 of the best predictor of YYY given XXX.

Orthogonality

If E[Y∣X]=0E[Y \mid X] = 0E[Y∣X]=0, then YYY is uncorrelated with every function of XXX. Iterated expectations again:

E[g(X) Y]=E[g(X) E[Y∣X]]=0E\big[g(X)\, Y\big] = E\big[g(X)\, E[Y \mid X]\big] = 0E[g(X)Y]=E[g(X)E[Y∣X]]=0

Note

This is where the OLS orthogonality conditions come from. Strict exogeneity, E[u∣X]=0E[u \mid X] = 0E[u∣X]=0, makes the error orthogonal to the regressors and to any function of them.

Takeaway

E[Y∣X]E[Y \mid X]E[Y∣X] is a random variable, and averaging it returns E[Y]E[Y]E[Y]. Total spread is spread within groups plus spread between them. A zero conditional mean makes YYY orthogonal to everything built from XXX.

5. Independence, mean independence and correlation

Three ways for YYY to be unrelated to XXX, from strongest to weakest:

  • Independent: the joint density factors, f(x,y)=fX(x) fY(y)f(x, y) = f_X(x)\, f_Y(y)f(x,y)=fX​(x)fY​(y). Knowing XXX tells you nothing about YYY.
  • Mean independent: E[Y∣X]=E[Y]E[Y \mid X] = E[Y]E[Y∣X]=E[Y]. Knowing XXX tells you nothing about the mean of YYY.
  • Uncorrelated: Cov⁡(X,Y)=0\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0Cov(X,Y)=0. No linear relation.

Each implies the next, and neither arrow reverses.

  • Uncorrelated but dependent. Take X∼N(0,1)X \sim N(0, 1)X∼N(0,1) and Y=X2Y = X^2Y=X2. Then Cov⁡(X,Y)=E[X3]−E[X]E[X2]=0\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[X^3] - E[X]E[X^2] = 0Cov(X,Y)=E[X3]−E[X]E[X2]=0, yet YYY is a function of XXX.
  • Mean independent but dependent. Take Y=σ(X) εY = \sigma(X)\,\varepsilonY=σ(X)ε with ε\varepsilonε independent of XXX and E[ε]=0E[\varepsilon] = 0E[ε]=0. Then E[Y∣X]=0E[Y \mid X] = 0E[Y∣X]=0, but the spread of YYY moves with XXX. This is heteroskedasticity.

The exception is joint normality: if (X,Y)(X, Y)(X,Y) is jointly normal and uncorrelated, XXX and YYY are independent. Jointly matters. With X∼N(0,1)X \sim N(0, 1)X∼N(0,1) and an independent random sign S=±1S = \pm 1S=±1, Y=SXY = SXY=SX is normal and uncorrelated with XXX, yet ∣Y∣=∣X∣|Y| = |X|∣Y∣=∣X∣ always.

Takeaway

Independence, then mean independence, then zero covariance. Only under joint normality does the chain run backwards. Correlation measures straight line association and nothing more.

6. Testing distributions and quantiles

The distributions used for tests are all built from independent standard normals.

  • If Z∼N(0,1)Z \sim N(0, 1)Z∼N(0,1), then Z2∼χ12Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1Z2∼χ12​, and the sum of kkk independent such squares is χk2\chi^2_kχk2​.
  • With Z∼N(0,1)Z \sim N(0,1)Z∼N(0,1) independent of V∼χk2V \sim \chi^2_kV∼χk2​: tk=Z/V/kt_k = Z / \sqrt{V/k}tk​=Z/V/k​.
  • With independent V1∼χm2V_1 \sim \chi^2_mV1​∼χm2​ and V2∼χk2V_2 \sim \chi^2_kV2​∼χk2​: Fm,k=(V1/m)/(V2/k)F_{m,k} = (V_1/m)/(V_2/k)Fm,k​=(V1​/m)/(V2​/k). Squaring a tkt_ktk​ gives an F1,kF_{1,k}F1,k​.

The α\alphaα quantile is qα=inf⁡{x:F(x)≥α}q_\alpha = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge \alpha\}qα​=inf{x:F(x)≥α}. When FFF is continuous and strictly increasing this is just the solution of F(qα)=αF(q_\alpha) = \alphaF(qα​)=α.

The probability integral transform: if FFF is continuous, then F(X)∼U(0,1)F(X) \sim U(0, 1)F(X)∼U(0,1). For strictly increasing FFF the proof is one line:

P(F(X)≤u)=P(X≤F−1(u))=F(F−1(u))=u\begin{aligned} P\big(F(X) \le u\big) &= P\big(X \le F^{-1}(u)\big) \\ &= F\big(F^{-1}(u)\big) = u \end{aligned}P(F(X)≤u)​=P(X≤F−1(u))=F(F−1(u))=u​

Run it backwards and F−1(U)F^{-1}(U)F−1(U) with U∼U(0,1)U \sim U(0,1)U∼U(0,1) has distribution FFF. That is how computers draw from any distribution.

Takeaway

χ2\chi^2χ2, ttt and FFF are built from independent standard normals. Passing a continuous variable through its own CDF gives a uniform, which is why exact p values exist and why simulation works.
