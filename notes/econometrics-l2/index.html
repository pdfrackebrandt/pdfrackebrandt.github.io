Estimation, asymptotics and confidence intervals

What an estimator is, why the sample mean converges, when the average becomes normal, and how to build intervals that cover the truth with high probability.

All notes

1. Estimators and the analogy principle

A population quantity (mean, variance, CDF) is a function of an unknown distribution. The data are draws from that distribution, so sample analogues carry information about it. An estimator is a function of the random sample, hence a random variable. An estimate is the number it produces in one particular sample.

The analogy principle replaces each expectation by a sample average. The sample mean $\bar X_n = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is the expectation under the empirical distribution that puts mass $1/n$ on each observation. The same idea gives the sample variance and the empirical CDF $F_n(x) = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n 1\{X_i \le x\}$.

Takeaway

An estimator is a random variable. Everything that follows describes its distribution, exactly at fixed $n$ or approximately as $n$ grows.

2. Law of large numbers

The weak LLN says $\bar X_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$ when $E|X| < \infty$. The strong LLN says $\bar X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu$ under the same condition. If the mean does not exist (Cauchy), averaging fails and the path does not settle.

Note

The assumptions for the weak and strong LLN are the same. The difference is the mode of convergence.

3. Central limit theorem and Slutsky

The CLT says that if $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 < \infty$, then $$\sqrt{n}(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1).$$ Standardised means $(\bar X_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$. Slutsky lets you replace any consistent estimator inside a continuous function without changing the limiting distribution. That is how we get asymptotic standard errors and confidence intervals.

Takeaway

LLN tells you the average converges. CLT tells you the rate and the shape. Slutsky lets you plug in estimated variances.

4. Consistency versus unbiasedness

Unbiasedness is a finite-sample property: $E[\hat\theta_n] = \theta$ for every $n$. Consistency is asymptotic: $\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta$. An estimator can be biased for every finite $n$ yet consistent (the bias shrinks). It can also be unbiased yet inconsistent if its variance does not shrink.

Example: the sample variance with divisor $n$ is biased but consistent. The sample variance with divisor $n-1$ is unbiased (under normality) and also consistent.
